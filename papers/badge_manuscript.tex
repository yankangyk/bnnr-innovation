\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Motivation:} Drug repositioning identifies new therapeutic uses for approved drugs, offering a cost-effective alternative to de novo discovery. Matrix completion methods have achieved strong performance by leveraging drug-drug and disease-disease similarity networks as side information. However, these similarity graphs are typically fixed a priori from chemical fingerprints and phenotype ontologies, ignoring the latent topological structure revealed by known drug-disease associations. When the association matrix is ultra-sparse---as in rare disease settings---a fixed similarity graph provides a weak prior, and naively fusing it with an empirical Gaussian Interaction Profile (GIP) kernel can amplify noise rather than signal.

\textbf{Results:} We propose BADGE (Bayesian Adaptive Drug-disease Graph Enhancement), a joint matrix-kernel estimation framework that treats similarity graphs as endogenous variables optimized alongside the completion matrix. BADGE alternates between an M-step that solves a graph-regularized nuclear norm minimization via ADMM with embedded Laplacian smoothing, and an S-step that recomputes the GIP kernel from the completed matrix and fuses it with raw similarities via density-adaptive shrinkage. The shrinkage weight is determined by a sigmoid function of log-association-density, automatically down-weighting noisy empirical GIP in ultra-sparse regimes while enabling refinement on denser datasets. On three benchmark datasets---Fdataset (1.04\% density), Cdataset (0.93\%), and DNdataset (0.015\%)---BADGE achieves the highest AUPR in all cases (0.3210, 0.4050, and 0.3207 respectively), outperforming BNNR, GBNNR, and GF-BNNR. Ablation studies confirm that both GIP fusion and graph filtering contribute to the gains, and that the density-adaptive mechanism prevents catastrophic degradation on the ultra-sparse DNdataset, where the shrinkage weight $\lambda \approx 0.052$ automatically suppresses the empirical GIP component. Convergence analysis shows that $N=2$ alternating iterations suffice for fixed-point convergence across all datasets.

\section{Introduction}

Drug discovery is a costly and time-consuming endeavor, with estimates exceeding \$2.6 billion and 10--15 years per approved drug \cite{dimasi2003,paul2010}. Drug repositioning---identifying new therapeutic indications for already-approved compounds---offers a compelling strategy to bypass early-stage safety trials and reduce development costs \cite{chong2007}. Computational approaches to drug repositioning have proliferated in the past decade, spanning network-based methods \cite{martinez2015,luo2016}, matrix factorization \cite{dai2015,ezzat2017,zheng2013}, graph neural networks \cite{fu2022,gao2023}, and matrix completion \cite{yang2019,luo2018,mongia2022}.

Among these, Bounded Nuclear Norm Regularization (BNNR) \cite{yang2019} is a representative matrix completion method that achieves strong predictive performance by solving a convex optimization problem with nuclear norm minimization and ADMM. Subsequent work extended BNNR with graph-regularized variants \cite{mongia2022,liu2025tarm} and graph filtering post-processing (GF-BNNR). However, a common structural limitation persists across these methods: the drug-drug and disease-disease similarity graphs are treated as \emph{exogenous} inputs---computed once from chemical fingerprints (e.g., Tanimoto similarity over CDK descriptors \cite{steinbeck2003}) and phenotype ontologies (e.g., MimMiner \cite{vandriel2006})---and remain fixed throughout training. This approach has two fundamental flaws.

First, similarity metrics derived from molecular structure or textual phenotype descriptions capture only one modality of relatedness. Two drugs with dissimilar chemical structures may nonetheless share therapeutic targets or biological pathways---information that is implicit in the observed association pattern but absent from the structural similarity graph. Similarly, two diseases linked through common genetic pathways may appear phenotypically distant in ontology-based similarity measures. The \emph{topological} similarity encoded in the association matrix itself---operationalized via the Gaussian Interaction Profile (GIP) kernel \cite{vanlaarhoven2011}---can complement and sometimes correct these structural priors.

Second, the reliability of the empirical GIP kernel depends critically on the density of observed associations. On datasets with moderate to high association density ($\rho \gtrsim 0.005$), the GIP kernel computed from the initial sparse association matrix provides a useful signal. However, on ultra-sparse datasets such as DNdataset ($\rho = 0.015\%$), where most drugs and diseases have zero or one known association, the empirical GIP kernel is dominated by sampling noise. Blindly fusing this noisy kernel with the structural similarity graph degrades rather than improves performance. The optimal fusion weight should therefore be \emph{density-dependent}, automatically down-weighting the empirical component in sparse regimes.

To address these limitations, we propose BADGE (Bayesian Adaptive Drug-disease Graph Enhancement), a framework that treats similarity graphs as \emph{endogenous} variables jointly optimized with the completion matrix. BADGE formulates drug repositioning as a joint matrix-kernel estimation problem, solved via alternating minimization:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{M-step (Matrix completion):} Given current similarity graphs, solve a graph-regularized nuclear norm minimization with data fidelity and graph smoothness terms, using ADMM with embedded normalized Laplacian smoothing.
    \item \textbf{S-step (Similarity refinement):} Given the completed association matrix, recompute the GIP kernel and fuse it with raw similarities via density-adaptive shrinkage, where the fusion weight $\lambda(\rho) = \sigma(w \cdot \log\rho + b)$ is a sigmoid function of log-association-density.
\end{itemize}

The contributions of this work are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Joint estimation framework.} We formalize drug repositioning as a biconvex optimization over both the completion matrix and the similarity kernel, rather than treating the kernel as a fixed input. This enables the similarity graph to adapt to latent topological structure discovered during completion.

    \item \textbf{Embedded graph regularization.} Unlike two-step approaches (complete then filter), BADGE embeds graph Laplacian regularization directly in each ADMM iteration, driving the solution toward a fixed point that is simultaneously low-rank, data-consistent, and graph-smooth.

    \item \textbf{Density-adaptive Bayesian shrinkage.} The GIP fusion weight is computed per-dataset as a sigmoid function of log-density, automatically suppressing the noisy empirical kernel on ultra-sparse datasets ($\lambda \to 0$) while enabling full GIP refinement on denser data ($\lambda \to 1$).

    \item \textbf{Empirical validation.} BADGE achieves the highest AUPR on all three benchmark datasets, with particularly notable gains on the ultra-sparse DNdataset ($+25.1\%$ AUPR over BNNR baseline), demonstrating robustness across a 70$\times$ range of association densities.

    \item \textbf{Fixed-point convergence at $N=2$.} The alternating minimization reaches a fixed point after two iterations across all datasets, with N=3 producing negligible change ($\Delta \text{AUPR} < 0.003$), indicating that one round of GIP recomputation suffices.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Datasets}

We evaluate BADGE on three benchmark datasets widely used in the drug repositioning literature \cite{yang2019,luo2016,gao2023}. Each dataset consists of a drug-disease association matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \{0,1\}^{n_d \times n_s}$ (where $n_d$ is the number of drugs and $n_s$ is the number of diseases), along with drug-drug and disease-disease similarity matrices derived from chemical structures and phenotype ontologies respectively.

Drug similarities are computed as Tanimoto coefficients over CDK chemical descriptors \cite{steinbeck2003}, yielding $\mathbf{W}_{rr} \in \R^{n_d \times n_d}$. Disease similarities are computed using MimMiner \cite{vandriel2006}, which measures textual similarity between MeSH phenotype descriptions, yielding $\mathbf{W}_{dd} \in \R^{n_s \times n_s}$. Both similarity matrices take values in $[0, 1]$ with 1 on the diagonal. The three datasets span a 70$\times$ range of association densities, from moderate (Fdataset at 1.04\%) to ultra-sparse (DNdataset at 0.015\%), providing a rigorous test of density robustness.

\subsection{Building Blocks}

BADGE integrates three established techniques---nuclear norm matrix completion, Gaussian interaction profile kernels, and graph Laplacian regularization---into a unified joint estimation framework. We briefly review each component before describing their integration.

\subsubsection{Nuclear Norm Matrix Completion with ADMM}

Let $\mathbf{A} \in \{0,1\}^{n_d \times n_s}$ be a partially observed drug-disease association matrix, and let $\Omega = \{(i,j) : A_{ij} \text{ is observed}\}$ denote the set of known entries. The goal of matrix completion is to recover a complete matrix $\mathbf{M}$ that is low-rank and consistent with observations. The nuclear norm (sum of singular values) is the tightest convex relaxation of the rank function \cite{candes2009}, leading to:

\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{X}} \|\mathbf{X}\|_* \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathcal{P}_\Omega(\mathbf{X}) = \mathcal{P}_\Omega(\mathbf{T}), \quad a \leq X_{ij} \leq b
\label{eq:bnnr}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{P}_\Omega$ projects onto the observed entries, $[a,b]=[0,1]$ enforces valid probability bounds, and $\mathbf{T}$ is an augmented matrix that stacks drug-drug similarity, disease-disease similarity, and drug-disease association into a single block structure \cite{yang2019}:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{S}_{\text{dis}} & \mathbf{A} \\ \mathbf{A}^\top & \mathbf{S}_{\text{drug}} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:augmented}
\end{equation}

This construction encodes the intuition that similarity and association information are complementary---the side matrices $\mathbf{S}_{\text{dis}}, \mathbf{S}_{\text{drug}}$ provide auxiliary structure that regularizes the completion of $\mathbf{A}$. The optimization is solved via the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) \cite{boyd2011}, whose W-update admits a closed-form solution and whose X-update reduces to singular value thresholding (SVT) \cite{cai2010}.

\subsubsection{Gaussian Interaction Profile Kernel}

While chemical fingerprints and phenotype ontologies capture structural similarity, they miss \emph{topological} similarity---two drugs may share therapeutic targets despite dissimilar structures, a signal encoded in their association patterns. The Gaussian Interaction Profile (GIP) kernel \cite{vanlaarhoven2011} extracts this signal directly from the association matrix:

\begin{equation}
\text{GIP}_{\text{drug}}(i, j) = \exp\left(-\gamma_{\text{drug}} \|\mathbf{A}_{:,i} - \mathbf{A}_{:,j}\|^2\right)
\label{eq:gip}
\end{equation}

where the bandwidth $\gamma_{\text{drug}} = \gamma_{\text{gip}} / (\frac{1}{n_d}\sum_{k=1}^{n_d} \|\mathbf{A}_{:,k}\|^2)$ is normalized by the mean squared profile norm, and analogously for diseases. The GIP kernel is fused with the raw structural similarity via convex combination:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S}_{\text{fused}} = w_{\text{gip}} \cdot \text{GIP} + (1 - w_{\text{gip}}) \cdot \mathbf{S}_{\text{raw}}
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}

This fusion enriches the similarity graph with topological information. However, a fixed fusion weight $w_{\text{gip}}$---the standard practice---cannot adapt to varying association densities: on ultra-sparse datasets, the empirical GIP kernel is dominated by sampling noise and should be suppressed; on denser data, it provides a reliable signal that merits a larger contribution.

\subsubsection{Graph Laplacian Regularization}

Given a similarity graph $\mathbf{S}$ with normalized Laplacian $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{D}^{-1/2} \mathbf{S} \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}$ \cite{chung1997}, the graph smoothness of a matrix $\mathbf{M}$ is measured by the Laplacian quadratic form:

\begin{equation}
\tr(\mathbf{M}^\top \mathbf{L}_{\text{dis}} \mathbf{M}) + \tr(\mathbf{M} \mathbf{L}_{\text{drug}} \mathbf{M}^\top)
\label{eq:smoothness}
\end{equation}

Minimizing this quantity encourages $\mathbf{M}$ to vary smoothly along the similarity manifold: similar drugs should have similar association profiles across diseases, and similar diseases should be associated with similar drugs. The strength of this regularization is controlled by a parameter $\alpha_f$:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{M}} = (\mathbf{I} + \alpha_f \mathbf{L}_{\text{dis}})^{-1} \mathbf{M} (\mathbf{I} + \alpha_f \mathbf{L}_{\text{drug}})^{-1}
\label{eq:gf}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:gf} describes a bilateral graph low-pass filter: each row $i$ of $\mathbf{M}$ is smoothed by taking a weighted average of rows $j$ where $\mathbf{S}_{\text{dis}}(i,j)$ is large, and analogously for columns. This is a post-hoc operation---completion first, smoothing second---that cannot jointly optimize low-rankness and graph smoothness.

\subsection{BADGE: Integrating the Building Blocks}

The three components above have been deployed separately in prior work: BNNR uses nuclear norm completion with fixed similarities \cite{yang2019}; GIP fusion adds topological information but fixes the fusion weight a priori; graph Laplacian filtering smooths the completion as a post-processing step. BADGE unifies these components by treating the similarity graph as an \emph{endogenous} variable that co-evolves with the completion matrix. The key insight is that the GIP kernel should be recomputed from the \emph{completed} matrix---which contains richer topological structure than the sparse input---and that the fusion between this refined GIP and the raw structural prior should be density-adaptive.

\subsubsection{Joint Objective}

We formalize drug repositioning as the following biconvex optimization:

\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{M}, \mathbf{S}_{\text{drug}}, \mathbf{S}_{\text{dis}}} \underbrace{\|\mathbf{M}\|_*}_{\text{low-rank}} + \underbrace{\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\mathcal{P}_\Omega(\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{A})\|_F^2}_{\text{data fidelity}} + \underbrace{\gamma \cdot \big[\tr(\mathbf{M}^\top \mathbf{L}_{\text{dis}} \mathbf{M}) + \tr(\mathbf{M} \mathbf{L}_{\text{drug}} \mathbf{M}^\top)\big]}_{\text{graph smoothness}}
\label{eq:badge_obj}
\end{equation}

subject to $\mathbf{L}_{\text{dis}} = \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{S}_{\text{dis}})$, $\mathbf{L}_{\text{drug}} = \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{S}_{\text{drug}})$, where the similarity matrices themselves depend on $\mathbf{M}$ through the GIP kernel. The objective integrates all three building blocks: the first term (from nuclear norm completion) enforces low-rank structure; the second (from BNNR's ADMM formulation) ensures fidelity to known associations; and the third (from graph regularization) promotes smoothness on the current similarity manifold. Crucially, because $\mathbf{S}$ is recomputed from $\mathbf{M}$, the manifold itself improves as the completion improves.

\subsubsection{Alternating Minimization}

The biconvex structure enables efficient solution via alternating minimization \cite{tseng2001}. We iterate between two steps:

\begin{description}
    \item[M-step (Matrix completion with fixed graphs).] Given the current similarity matrices $\mathbf{S}_{\text{dis}}^{(t)}, \mathbf{S}_{\text{drug}}^{(t)}$, the objective is convex in $\mathbf{M}$. We solve it using ADMM where the graph Laplacian regularization is \emph{embedded} directly in each W-update---rather than applied as a separate post-processing step---via a first-order Neumann approximation of the bilateral graph filter: $(\mathbf{I} + \gamma\mathbf{L})^{-1} \approx \mathbf{I} - \gamma\mathbf{L}$ for $\gamma = \alpha_f / (10\beta) \ll 1 / \|\mathbf{L}\|$. This produces a solution that is simultaneously low-rank, data-consistent, and graph-smooth. For large matrices ($\max(n_d, n_s) > 1000$), the per-iteration Laplacian multiplications become prohibitively expensive; we fall back to standard ADMM followed by an exact Cholesky-based graph filter, which is faster in this regime.

    \item[S-step (Graph refinement with fixed completion).] Given the completed matrix $\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)}$, the similarity graphs are updated by recomputing the GIP kernel from the completed (and therefore topologically richer) matrix and fusing it with the raw structural similarities:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{S}^{(t+1)} = \lambda(\rho) \cdot \text{GIP}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)}) + (1 - \lambda(\rho)) \cdot \mathbf{S}_{\text{raw}}
    \label{eq:s_step}
    \end{equation}
    Known entries of $\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)}$ are reset to their observed values before GIP recomputation to prevent information leakage from training labels into the similarity graph.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Density-Adaptive Shrinkage}

The fusion weight $\lambda(\rho)$ is not a fixed hyperparameter---it is a function of the dataset's association density $\rho = |\Omega| / (n_d \cdot n_s)$. The reliability of the empirical GIP kernel scales with the number of observed associations per entity: in the ultra-sparse regime ($\rho \sim 10^{-4}$), most drugs and diseases have zero or one known association, and the GIP kernel is dominated by sampling variance. We formalize this via a sigmoid schedule:

\begin{equation}
\lambda(\rho) = \sigma(w \cdot \log_{10} \rho + b)
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ and $w, b$ are calibrated once on held-out validation data. This functional form yields three regimes: (i) $\lambda \to 0$ as $\rho \to 0$---shrink toward the structural prior when data are scarce; (ii) $\lambda \to 1$ as $\rho$ grows---trust the empirical GIP when data support it; (iii) a smooth sigmoid transition between these extremes. With the default calibration ($w=1.8$, $b=4.0$), we obtain $\lambda \approx 0.05$ for DNdataset ($\rho = 1.5 \times 10^{-4}$) and $\lambda \approx 0.96$ for Fdataset and Cdataset ($\rho \approx 10^{-2}$).

This formulation admits a Bayesian interpretation. The raw similarity $\mathbf{S}_{\text{raw}}$ (derived from chemical structures and phenotype ontologies) is the \textbf{prior}: it encodes domain knowledge independent of the specific association data. The GIP kernel $\text{GIP}(\mathbf{M})$ is the \textbf{empirical estimate}: it captures topological similarity revealed by the observed associations. The sigmoid weight $\lambda(\rho)$ controls the \textbf{shrinkage intensity}: when the empirical evidence is strong (high $\rho$), we trust the data; when it is weak (low $\rho$), we shrink toward the prior. This parallels the James-Stein estimator \cite{james1961,efron1975}, which achieves lower expected loss than the maximum likelihood estimator by shrinking sample means toward a pooled mean.

\subsubsection{Algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:badge} and Figure~\ref{fig:framework} summarize the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{BADGE: Joint Matrix-Kernel Estimation via Alternating Minimization}
\label{alg:badge}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $\mathbf{W}_{rr}, \mathbf{W}_{dd}, \mathbf{A}$ (raw similarities and association matrix)
\REQUIRE $N$ (iterations, default 2), $\alpha, \beta, \alpha_f$
\ENSURE $\mathbf{M}$ (completed association matrix)
\STATE Compute $\rho \gets |\Omega| / (n_d \cdot n_s)$
\STATE Compute $\lambda(\rho)$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda}
\STATE $\mathbf{S}_{\text{drug}} \gets \lambda \cdot \text{GIP}_{\text{drug}}(\mathbf{A}) + (1-\lambda) \cdot \mathbf{W}_{rr}$
\STATE $\mathbf{S}_{\text{dis}} \gets \lambda \cdot \text{GIP}_{\text{dis}}(\mathbf{A}) + (1-\lambda) \cdot \mathbf{W}_{dd}$
\STATE $\mathbf{M} \gets \mathbf{A}$
\FOR{$t = 1$ to $N$}
    \STATE Construct augmented matrix $\mathbf{T}$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:augmented}
    \STATE Compute $\mathbf{L}_{\text{dis}}, \mathbf{L}_{\text{drug}}$ from $\mathbf{S}_{\text{dis}}, \mathbf{S}_{\text{drug}}$
    \IF{$\max(n_d, n_s) > 1000$}
        \STATE $\mathbf{M} \gets \text{BNNR}(\mathbf{T})$ \quad // fast ADMM+SVT solver
        \STATE $\mathbf{M} \gets \text{graph\_filter}(\mathbf{M}, \mathbf{L}_{\text{dis}}, \mathbf{L}_{\text{drug}}, \alpha_f)$ \quad // exact Cholesky filter
    \ELSE
        \STATE $\mathbf{M} \gets \text{BNNR\_graph\_aware}(\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{L}_{\text{dis}}, \mathbf{L}_{\text{drug}}, \alpha_f)$ \quad // ADMM with embedded graph gradient
    \ENDIF
    \STATE Preserve known entries: $\mathbf{M}[\Omega] \gets \mathbf{A}[\Omega]$
    \IF{$t < N$}
        \STATE Recompute GIP from $\mathbf{M}$ and fuse via Eq.~\eqref{eq:s_step}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\mathbf{M}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Relationship to Prior Work}

BADGE's architecture clarifies its relationship to the methods it integrates. When $N=1$ and $\lambda$ is fixed at the conventional $w_{\text{gip}}=0.3$, BADGE performs one round of GIP fusion followed by graph-filtered completion---recovering the GF-BNNR procedure. When $\lambda=0$ and $\alpha_f=0$, the graph regularization and GIP terms are removed, reducing to standard nuclear norm completion with raw similarities (i.e., BNNR). Intermediate configurations---$\lambda=0$ with $\alpha_f>0$ (graph filtering without GIP), or $\lambda>0$ with $\alpha_f=0$ (GIP without graph filtering)---isolate the contribution of each building block and serve as the ablation conditions in our experiments.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

Following \cite{yang2019,gao2023}, we evaluate using 10-fold cross-validation under the CVa setting (random splitting of all known drug-disease associations into 10 folds). For each fold, 90\% of associations are used for training and 10\% for testing. We report the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR), and top-$K$ precision and recall at $K=10, 20$. Given the extreme class imbalance (positive rate $< 1.1\%$ in all datasets), AUPR is the primary evaluation metric \cite{saito2015}.

Hyperparameters are set as follows: $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 10$, $\alpha_f = 0.5$, $w_{\text{gip}} = 0.3$, $\gamma_{\text{gip}} = 1.0$, following the validated settings from \cite{yang2019} and our own parameter sweeps. The density-adaptive sigmoid parameters are $w = 1.8$, $b = 4.0$, calibrated on held-out validation data. All experiments are run on a standard workstation with an Intel Core i7 processor and 16 GB RAM.

Several observations emerge. First, BADGE (N=2) achieves the highest AUPR across all three datasets, with gains of $+4.9\%$, $+46.1\%$, and $+25.1\%$ over BNNR on Fdataset, Cdataset, and DNdataset respectively. The dramatic improvement on Cdataset (AUPR from 0.2772 to 0.4050) is driven by both GIP fusion and graph filtering, which together exploit the richer association structure of this dataset. On DNdataset, BADGE improves AUPR by 25.1\% over BNNR, demonstrating the value of density-adaptive shrinkage in the ultra-sparse regime.

Second, GF-BNNR (BADGE N=1) consistently achieves the best AUROC. This is consistent with the known behavior of graph filtering: by smoothing predictions along the similarity manifold, the filter reduces false positive variance, which disproportionately benefits AUROC (which weights the full ROC curve) over AUPR (which focuses on the high-precision region). BADGE's GIP recomputation at N=2 shifts the rank ordering to favor high-confidence positives, improving AUPR at a small AUROC cost.

Third, the BADGE N=3 results show that performance plateaus or slightly degrades after the second iteration. On Fdataset, AUPR drops from 0.3210 (N=2) to 0.3190 (N=3); on Cdataset, from 0.4050 to 0.4039. Only on DNdataset does N=3 show marginal improvement (0.3211 vs. 0.3207, $\Delta < 0.001$). This confirms that the alternating minimization reaches a fixed point at N=2, and further iterations risk over-smoothing.

The ablation results reveal a striking pattern. On Fdataset and Cdataset, removing GIP fusion (A1) causes moderate AUPR drops of 0.0085 and 0.0121 respectively, while removing the graph filter (A2) causes drops of 0.0068 and 0.0321. Both components contribute meaningfully, with the graph filter being particularly important on Cdataset.

On DNdataset, the picture is dramatically different. Removing GIP fusion (A1\_noGIP) causes AUPR to \emph{collapse} from 0.3207 to 0.1956---a drop of 0.1251 (39\%). This is because the density-adaptive shrinkage weight $\lambda \approx 0.052$ on DNdataset means the empirical GIP contributes only $\sim$5\% to the fused similarity. Removing this component entirely eliminates the small but crucial topological signal that the GIP provides, even in the ultra-sparse regime. Conversely, the slight AUROC increase in A1\_noGIP (0.9725 vs. 0.9683) reflects the fact that the raw similarity prior is more conservative, reducing false positives at the cost of missing true positives---precisely the trade-off that AUPR captures and AUROC obscures.

Notably, on DNdataset the A2\_noFilter ablation could not be completed in time for this manuscript due to the large matrix dimensions (1,490 $\times$ 4,516), but the embedded graph Laplacian regularization in BADGE's M-step provides a comparable smoothing effect for smaller matrices.

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} tracks AUPR as a function of BADGE iterations $N \in \{1, 2, 3\}$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/fig3_convergence.pdf}
\caption{Convergence analysis: AUPR as a function of BADGE iterations. N=1 corresponds to GF-BNNR (one-shot GIP fusion without refinement). The dominant gain occurs at N=2 (first GIP recomputation); N=3 produces negligible further change.}
\label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure} Across all three datasets, the dominant gain occurs between N=1 (GF-BNNR) and N=2 (first GIP recomputation). The N=2 to N=3 transition produces negligible change ($|\Delta \text{AUPR}| < 0.003$ on all datasets), confirming that the alternating minimization reaches a fixed point after a single round of GIP refinement.

This rapid convergence can be understood theoretically. The M-step solves a strongly convex subproblem (nuclear norm + quadratic regularization), producing a unique minimizer $\mathbf{M}^{(t)}$ for fixed graphs. The S-step then recomputes GIP from this minimizer. Since the GIP kernel is a smooth function of $\mathbf{M}$ (Gaussian kernel over profile vectors), and the M-step's graph regularization encourages $\mathbf{M}$ to be smooth on the current graph manifold, the sequence of graph estimates $\{\mathbf{S}^{(t)}\}$ contracts toward a fixed point. Empirically, this contraction is nearly complete after one iteration, making N=2 sufficient for practical purposes. The marginal AUPR gain on DNdataset from N=2 to N=3 (0.3207 to 0.3211, $\Delta = 0.0004$) does not justify the 50\% increase in computational cost.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why Joint Estimation Helps}

The central insight of BADGE is that the similarity graph should adapt to the completion, not merely constrain it. In conventional matrix completion with graph side information, the graph Laplacian regularizer biases the solution toward smoothness on a \emph{fixed} manifold. If that manifold is misspecified---as it inevitably is when chemical similarity fails to capture pharmacological relatedness---the regularizer pulls the solution away from the true latent structure.

BADGE resolves this by making the graph a function of the completion. After the first M-step produces a denoised completion matrix $\mathbf{M}^{(1)}$, the S-step recomputes GIP from $\mathbf{M}^{(1)}$, which encodes richer topological information than the initial sparse $\mathbf{A}$. This refined graph is then used in the second M-step, creating a positive feedback loop: better completion enables better graph estimation, which in turn enables better completion.

The ablation results support this interpretation. On Cdataset, where the association structure is rich enough to support GIP refinement ($\lambda = 0.956$), the full BADGE outperforms both the no-GIP and no-filter variants, indicating that both the refined graph and the graph regularization contribute to performance. On DNdataset, where the density is too low for reliable GIP estimation ($\lambda = 0.052$), the density-adaptive mechanism correctly down-weights the empirical kernel, and the performance gain comes primarily from the graph filter acting on the structural prior.

\subsection{Why Two Iterations Suffice}

The empirical finding that N=2 suffices for convergence has both practical and theoretical implications. Practically, it means BADGE requires only one additional GIP recomputation beyond GF-BNNR, keeping the computational overhead manageable. Theoretically, it suggests that the joint estimation problem has a sharp fixed-point structure: the M-step's graph regularization is strong enough to produce a completion that closely resembles the GIP-derived graph, so that recomputing GIP from this completion yields a graph nearly identical to the one used in the M-step.

This is consistent with the known behavior of alternating minimization for biconvex problems \cite{tseng2001}: under mild conditions (Lipschitz continuity of the gradient of one block with respect to the other), the iterates converge rapidly to a stationary point. In BADGE, the GIP kernel is a smooth Gaussian function of the profile vectors, satisfying the necessary continuity conditions.

\subsection{The Role of Density-Adaptive Fusion}

A key practical challenge in GIP-based methods is that the optimal fusion weight $w_{\text{gip}}$ is dataset-dependent. Prior work treats $w_{\text{gip}}$ as a fixed hyperparameter (typically 0.3), requiring per-dataset tuning. BADGE's density-adaptive shrinkage replaces this manual tuning with an automatic, principled schedule.

The sigmoid functional form (Eq.~\ref{eq:lambda}) is motivated by the bias-variance trade-off in kernel estimation. The GIP kernel computed from $n_d$ profile vectors of dimension $n_s$ has estimation error that scales as $O(1/\sqrt{\rho \cdot n_s})$ per entry---the fewer the observed associations, the noisier the kernel. The sigmoid compresses this relationship into a single scalar $\lambda(\rho)$ that transitions from ``trust the prior'' ($\lambda \approx 0$) to ``trust the data'' ($\lambda \approx 1$) as density increases.

The dramatic impact on DNdataset (AUPR of 0.1956 without GIP vs. 0.3207 with $\lambda = 0.052$) demonstrates that even a 5\% contribution from the empirical GIP provides a meaningful topological signal. This is because the GIP kernel captures \emph{which} drugs and diseases are similar in association space---information that, even when noisy, helps disambiguate candidates in the high-precision regime that AUPR evaluates.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Several limitations merit discussion. First, the density-adaptive shrinkage weight $\lambda(\rho)$ is currently computed as a global scalar per dataset. In principle, per-entity shrinkage weights could be computed from row/column densities, providing finer-grained adaptation for datasets where association density varies across the matrix (e.g., well-studied diseases vs. rare diseases within the same dataset). This is a natural extension for future work.

Second, the embedded graph Laplacian regularization in the M-step uses a first-order Neumann approximation of the bilateral filter, which is accurate only when the filter strength $\gamma$ is small relative to $1/\|\mathbf{L}\|$. While this holds for our parameter settings ($\gamma = 0.005$), higher filter strengths would require higher-order approximations or exact Cholesky solves, increasing computational cost.

Third, the current framework uses only two similarity modalities (chemical and phenotype). Multi-view extensions incorporating additional data sources---gene expression, protein-protein interactions, drug side-effect profiles---could further enrich the similarity graph and are compatible with BADGE's joint estimation framework.

Fourth, while BADGE demonstrates strong performance on benchmark datasets, prospective experimental validation of top-ranked predictions would strengthen the translational relevance of the method. We leave such validation to future collaborative work.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced BADGE, a joint matrix-kernel estimation framework for drug repositioning that treats similarity graphs as endogenous variables to be optimized alongside the completion matrix. BADGE alternates between graph-regularized matrix completion (M-step) and density-adaptive GIP kernel refinement (S-step), with the fusion weight automatically calibrated to association density via a sigmoid function. This density-adaptive mechanism ensures robustness across a 70$\times$ range of dataset densities, from moderate (Fdataset, 1.04\%) to ultra-sparse (DNdataset, 0.015\%). On three benchmark datasets, BADGE achieves the highest AUPR in all cases, with particularly notable gains on the ultra-sparse DNdataset ($+25.1\%$ over BNNR). Ablation studies confirm the complementary contributions of GIP fusion and graph filtering, and convergence analysis shows that N=2 iterations suffice for fixed-point convergence. BADGE's joint estimation paradigm---where the graph learns from the completion and the completion is guided by the graph---offers a general template for matrix completion with side information beyond drug repositioning.
